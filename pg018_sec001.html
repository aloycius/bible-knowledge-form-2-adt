Moses’ flight to Midian

Although Moses was brought up in the royal house and regarded as the son of

Pharaoh’s daughter, he was aware of his origin. When he grew up, he killed an

Egyptian overseer who was beating a Hebrew slave. In another occasion, he saw two Hebrews fighting, when he rebuked them, one of them said “…do you mean to kill me as you killed that Egyptian?’ (Exodus 2:14).

When Pharaoh heard of this murder story, he plotted to kill Moses. To rescue his life, Moses fled to Midian. While in Midian, his bravery won him a wife, Zipporah, a daughter of the priest of Midian. Moses got a wife after defending the seven daughters of Reu’el (Jethro) the priest of Midian from the shepherds who drove them away from the well. Zipporah bore Moses two sons. The first one was named

Gershom (meaning ‘I am a sojourner in a foreign land’, Exodus 2:11-22), the second son was Eliezer (meaning ‘the God of my father was my help’ Exodus 18:4).

Moses’ divine formation and mission

God prepared Moses for a special redemptive mission. He was adopted and raised in the house of the daughter of Pharaoh. He had the best education of the known ancient Egyptian world. Egypt was, at that time, one of the most productive and progressive countries of the known world, with the best educational achievements attained among the lands of the ancient world. Their economic and social life, too, was highly advanced. This was the environment in which Moses was raised up from his youth. A transition from the royal court of Pharaoh to the backside of the desert would have been a drastic change for anyone, but God had a purpose in Moses’ development. God was going to make Moses His instrument in the redemptive plan, though after some years of divine formation.

For forty years, Moses was in the courts of Pharaoh learning Egyptian wisdom, power structures and instruments of governance. Nevertheless, God had a different alternative through which His people were to be delivered from their slavery in

Egypt. Although Moses spent another forty years in Midian, tending his father-in-

law’s flock, he remained a common shepherd until when God finally changed the

cause of his life and leadership over his fellow countrymen and women. In other

words, Moses was exposed to a new divine call, reality, and mission that required

his personal submission to God rather than depending on his previous educational

prejudices or social status.
